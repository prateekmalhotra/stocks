Section 1: Business Model Reality & Revenue Engines

At its core, StoneCo Ltd. (STNE) is far more than a point-of-sale (POS) terminal provider or payment processor. It is an integrated financial technology platform operating at the intersection of merchant acquiring, short-term liquidity management, digital banking, and retail software in Brazil. To understand StoneCo’s economic machine, one must look past headline Total Payment Volume (TPV) and evaluate how the company monetizes the entire lifecycle of a Brazilian merchant’s cash flow.

1. Core Monetization Architecture & Revenue Streams

StoneCo generates revenue through four primary economic engines: Transaction Processing, Prepayment of Receivables (Financial Income), Banking Services, and Enterprise/Vertical Software.

The Mechanics of RAV (Recebíveis a Vencer) & Financial Income

In Brazil, standard credit card purchases are settled to merchants in 30 to 120 days depending on the number of installments (parcelado). However, small business owners operate with tight working capital and cannot wait months to pay suppliers or payroll. StoneCo steps into this liquidity gap by purchasing these future receivables at a discount—a process known as RAV (Recebíveis a Vencer).

StoneCo finances these advances through a combination of its own equity, bank loans, third-party debt issuances (such as FIDCs—Fundo de Investimento em Direitos Creditórios), and increasingly, low-cost retail deposits captured in the Stone Account. The gross financial spread—the difference between the discount rate charged to the merchant and StoneCo’s cost of funding—forms the bulk of the company's net earnings. When combined with the baseline MDR, StoneCo’s total effective take rate routinely sits well above pure transaction processing peers.

Software Realignment

Following a period of aggressive acquisitions, StoneCo rationalized its software strategy by divesting non-core software assets to sharpen focus on high-yield, vertical enterprise retail software (primarily through Linx). The core economic objective of software is not merely standalone SaaS margin, but embedding payment processing and banking natively into vertical POS systems (e.g., supermarkets, pharmacies, gas stations), locking out competing acquirers.

2. Customer Segmentation Breakdown

StoneCo addresses the Brazilian market through three distinct sub-brands, each tailored to a specific tier of the economic pyramid:

  • MSMBs (Stone Brand) – The Profit Core: Targeting micro, small, and medium businesses (annual TPV ranging from R$120,000 to R$15 million), the Stone brand is the economic engine of the company. Serviced directly by local Stone Hubs, these merchants generate high average revenue per client (ARPU) by adopting bundled packages: POS payment processing, RAV prepayment, Stone Account banking, and industry-specific POS software.
  • Micro-Entrepreneurs (Ton Brand): Aimed at street vendors, gig workers, and micro-entrepreneurs. Ton relies on digital-first acquisition, low hardware costs, and pay-as-you-go terminal sales. While individual client volumes are small, Ton operates at scale with minimal touch costs, charging higher effective take rates to offset lower individual merchant liquidity.
  • E-Commerce & Large Accounts (Pagar.me): Serving online retailers, digital platforms, and marketplaces via API-first infrastructure. Pagar.me delivers payment gateway, anti-fraud, and split-payment tools. StoneCo consolidated Pagar.me into its broader merchant platform to offer unified omnichannel capabilities, allowing merchants to run physical and online storefronts under a single financial ledger.

3. Macro Dynamics: Interest Rates, Instant Payments, and Credit Cycles

SELIC Interest Rate Sensitivity

The SELIC benchmark rate set by the Central Bank of Brazil (Banco Central do Brasil) is the single most critical exogenous variable for StoneCo. When SELIC sits at elevated levels (~14.00%), StoneCo’s nominal funding costs increase. To protect net margins, StoneCo must continuously reprice its RAV discount rates to merchants.
Conversely, a declining SELIC environment lowers funding costs rapidly across wholesale liabilities and credit facility lines. Because merchant pricing adjustments lag central bank rate cuts, monetary easing cycles historically drive temporary spread expansion for StoneCo. The growth of client deposits in the Stone Account (reaching ~R$10.1 billion) acts as a crucial structural buffer, reducing reliance on expensive third-party wholesale market debt.

The Pix Dynamics: Weapon or Threat?

The Central Bank's instant payment rail, Pix, has reshaped Brazilian payment habits. While Pix carries significantly lower merchant take rates than credit card transactions, it poses a nuanced economic tradeoff:

  • Debit Card Substitution: Pix has aggressively disintermediated low-margin debit card processing, reducing total card scheme interchange revenues.
  • P2M Volume Acceleration: Person-to-Merchant (P2M) Pix adoption brings previously cash-only informal commerce into the digital realm. StoneCo monetizes Pix P2M by charging small processing fees and utilizing instant settlement as an onboarding hook for banking accounts and working capital loans.
  • Capital Efficiency: Pix transactions settle instantaneously, requiring zero balance-sheet funding or prepayment capital, which yields a high return on capital despite lower absolute unit take rates.

Credit Cycle Realities & Underwriting Discipline

StoneCo’s expansion into merchant working capital lending represents significant upside accompanied by structural credit risk. Because StoneCo acts as the acquirer through which merchant daily sales flow, loan repayments are automatically deducted at the source (a mechanism known as trava de recebíveis or receivables lock).
While this structure reduces default risk compared to uncollateralized bank loans, macro headwinds, high baseline interest rates, and small business insolvency can cause non-performing loans (NPLs) to escalate. Credit loss provisions (Cost of Risk) fluctuate directly with broad macroeconomic health, requiring conservative risk modeling and disciplined underwriting to prevent balance-sheet impairment.

Section 2: Competitive Moat, Distribution Hubs & Market Dynamics

1. The "Stone Hub" Network: Operational Differentiation or OpEx Burden?

StoneCo’s primary competitive moat is built upon its proprietary, hyper-local distribution network known as Stone Hubs. Unlike traditional state-owned or private incumbent banks that rely on centralized corporate call centers and slow branch visits, StoneCo established hundreds of regional hubs across secondary and tertiary Brazilian cities.

Each Hub functions as an autonomous operational sales unit equipped with dedicated sales agents, logistics operations, and hyper-responsive customer service reps. Key operational SLAs include:

  • Hyper-Fast Customer Support: Telephone and WhatsApp support queries are answered by human representatives in approximately 5 seconds.
  • 1-Hour Physical Service SLA: If a POS terminal breaks down or experiences hardware faults, a green-clad local field agent delivers a functional replacement terminal directly to the merchant's storefront within 60 to 120 minutes.
  • Consultative Local Onboarding: Hub personnel establish personal relationships with local business owners, helping set up inventory management software, configure banking features, and optimize transaction flows.

Value Investor Reality Check: Skeptics frequently classify the Stone Hub model as an unnecessary overhead expense (OpEx drag) in a digital age. However, in the context of Brazilian MSMB commerce, high-touch local service generates tangible economic moats: lower customer churn, higher cross-sell velocity for high-margin banking and credit products, and superior pricing power relative to distant legacy bank acquirers.

3. Moat Durability, Churn Risks, and Structural Threats

Pricing Power vs. Commodity Commoditization

Basic debit and credit processing is fundamentally a commodity. Acquirers that compete solely on baseline Merchant Discount Rates (MDR) face relentless margin compression. StoneCo’s pricing power relies on structural bundling. A merchant utilizing StoneCo exclusively for card processing can easily switch to a competitor for a 5-basis-point discount. However, a merchant whose daily cash registers, inventory software (Linx), business checking account, and employee payroll are fully integrated into StoneCo faces high switching friction.

Customer Churn Mechanics in Brazil

Merchant churn in the Brazilian fintech landscape falls into two distinct categories:

  1. Economic Insolvency Churn: Macroeconomic volatility, high inflation, and high debt servicing costs periodically cause small business bankruptcies. This is an industry-wide macro factor rather than a product failure.
  2. Voluntary Switching Churn: Driven by price wars or poor customer service. StoneCo's Hub network and low support response times act as an effective shield against voluntary switching, outperforming legacy bank acquirers that suffer from high churn due to poor service levels.

Long-Term Threat of Central Bank Disintermediation

The ongoing roadmap of the Central Bank of Brazil presents long-term strategic considerations. As regulatory authorities introduce advanced functionalities to Pix (such as Pix Parcelado, which allows installment payments directly via instant bank rails without credit card schemes), traditional credit card acquiring revenues and interchange dynamics will adapt.
StoneCo’s long-term defense depends on evolving from a payment gateway into an indispensable operating system for small businesses. By controlling the software interface, client bank accounts, and localized business relationship, StoneCo captures value across merchant financial operations, regardless of whether payments flow through credit card rails, Pix, or future digital currency rails.

2. Treating SBC as a 100% Real Economic Expense

Standard Wall Street convention routine adds back Stock-Based Compensation as a "non-cash expense" to derive Adjusted EBITDA and Adjusted Net Income. As fundamental value investors, we reject this notion outright. SBC is a 100% real economic cost. Whether a firm pays its engineers and executives in cash or dilutes existing shareholders by issuing equity, the wealth transfer from existing owners to employees is identical.

  • Management Non-GAAP Distortion: In Q1 2026, management reported Adjusted Net Income of R$549.1 million, excluding R$37.1 million in stock compensation and amortized intangible adjustments. Over FY25, SBC totaled R$148.0 million.
  • True Owner Earnings Impact: Subtracting real SBC dilution costs and routine hardware maintenance CapEx (POS terminals and servers) from net income reduces true quarterly owner earnings to approximately R$385 million to R$420 million.
  • Adjusted Net Margin Compression: While management reported an Adjusted Net Margin of 15.3% in Q1 2026, true un-adjusted economic net margin sat closer to 12.8% when factoring in real share dilution and rising credit provisioning costs.

Section 4: Balance Sheet Dynamics, Receivables Funding & Net Cash Position

Analyzing a payment processor and merchant lender like StoneCo requires separating operational balance sheet liabilities (used to fund merchant prepayments and receivables) from corporate financial debt. Because merchant acquiring involves settling card transactions within 1 to 30 days, StoneCo uses specialized credit structure vehicles—primarily FIDCs (Fundos de Investimento em Direitos Creditórios), bank lines, and client digital banking deposits—to fund daily working capital.

1. Deconstructing the Fintech Balance Sheet

StoneCo's balance sheet consists of two distinct economic pillars:

  • Operational Receivables Funding (FIDC & Prepayment Obligations): When Brazilian merchants request early payment for credit card installments, StoneCo purchases these merchant receivables (Accounts Receivable from Card Issuers). StoneCo finances this through FIDC quotas sold to institutional investors and third-party bank lines. This structural leverage is operational, fully collateralized by central bank-registered card receivables, and self-liquidating.
  • Client Banking Deposits: Retail and merchant banking deposits reached R$10.1 billion in Q1 2026 (+21.9% YoY). This deposit base provides a low-cost, stable funding source that reduces StoneCo's reliance on expensive third-party wholesale debt, lowering funding costs to ~87% of the CDI benchmark rate.
  • Corporate Financial Debt: Long-term corporate bonds and bilateral bank loans used for general corporate obligations. Corporate debt maturities remain spread out beyond 2027–2028, minimizing immediate refinancing risk.
