Section 1: Business Model Reality & Unbreakable Membership Moat

Costco Wholesale Corporation operates one of the most powerful business flywheels in modern corporate history, built on the economic engine known as "Scale Economy Shared." Unlike traditional retailers that aim to maximize gross margins on individual merchandise sales, Costco deliberately caps its merchandise markups at ~11.0% (compared to 25%–35% for conventional supermarket and department store competitors). By pricing high-quality Kirkland Signature and national brand products near cost, Costco relinquishes short-term retail profit to pass maximum purchasing power directly back to its member base. In exchange, members pay a mandatory annual fee for access to these unbeatable prices, transforming the business from a traditional low-margin retailer into a high-margin recurring membership enterprise.

This self-reinforcing flywheel operates across a global footprint of 931 warehouses. Higher unit sales volume yields lower per-unit supplier costs; supplier cost savings are immediately passed to members via lower price tags; lower prices drive member retention, foot traffic, and new sign-ups; and expanding membership fees provide the capital to fund warehouse expansion, higher wages, and lower prices once again. Because merchandise gross margin ($7.64 billion in Q3 FY2026) is largely absorbed by warehouse operating costs, employee compensation, and logistics SG&A, membership fee income represents over 60% of Costco's total operating income over a full annual cycle—creating an unbreakable, bond-like cash flow floor that protects the business during macro downturns.

The true power of Costco's economic moat lies in the concentration and stickiness of its premium tier: Executive Memberships. Comprising 41.2 million paid accounts (~50% of total paid memberships), Executive Members drive approximately 75% of global net sales. Combined with an industry-leading 92.2% renewal rate in the U.S. and Canada (89.7% worldwide), Costco's membership engine functions as a tollbooth on consumer spending, generating multi-year visibility into cash returns regardless of short-term inflation or shifts in discretionary consumer sentiment.

1. Owner Earnings Reconciliation & CapEx Disaggregation

To calculate true Owner Earnings as defined in Berkshire Hathaway’s 1986 Shareholder Letter, we begin with Cash Flow from Operations ($11.13 billion through the first 36 weeks of fiscal 2026), subtract 100% of Stock-Based Compensation as a real cash charge, and subtract only Maintenance Capital Expenditures needed to preserve Costco’s existing store network and unit volume.

Out of Costco’s $4.23 billion in year-to-date capital expenditures, management directs approximately 67% ($2.83 billion) toward discretionary growth—specifically land acquisition, construction, and supply chain buildout for 25 to 30 new warehouse club openings per year globally. True maintenance CapEx (depleted equipment replacement, warehouse modernizations, IT infrastructure, and optical/gas station refurbishments) is estimated at ~$1.40 billion YTD (~$2.0B annualized). Deducting maintenance CapEx ($1.40B) and SBC ($652M) from operating cash flow ($11.13B) yields a normalized 36-week Owner Earnings figure of $9.08 billion.

2. The Negative Working Capital Engine & Interest-Free Float

Costco operates one of the most powerful working capital float mechanisms in global commerce. As outlined in Warren Buffett’s analysis of insurance float (1995 & 2002 Berkshire Letters), an enterprise that collects cash upfront while holding supplier payables interest-free creates an organic funding vehicle that compounds ROIC.

The Costco Float Mechanics: Costco turns over its inventory approximately 11.5 to 12 times per year (average inventory holding period of ~30 days). However, its credit terms with vendors allow payables to stretch out to 35–40 days. Consequently, Costco collects cash from members (or credit card processors within 24–48 hours) before paying suppliers for the inventory sold. Combined with $1.37 billion in quarterly membership fees collected 100% upfront in cash with near-zero marginal cost of goods sold, Costco runs a negative cash conversion cycle. This interest-free vendor and member float effectively funds warehouse inventory and initial store equipment, minimizing the need for expensive long-term debt or equity issuance.

3. SBC Dilution Audit & Share Repurchase Neutralization

Stock-Based Compensation is an uncompromised cost of doing business. Through the first 36 weeks of fiscal 2026, Costco recognized $652 million in non-cash equity compensation. Over the same period, management deployed $603 million in cash repurchases to buy back 638,000 common shares.

Costco’s diluted share count declined marginally from 444.8 million to 444.4 million shares YoY. The share repurchase program operates almost exclusively as an anti-dilution offset against RSUs and executive stock grants rather than a true "cannibal" compounding machine. Investors should not model aggressive share count reduction; buybacks serve as a protective shield to preserve current per-share owner earnings rather than expand them via share shrinkage.

1. Execution Risk Assessment

At 48x P/E, Costco's stock price leaves no room for operational missteps. Primary execution risks include:

  • Supply Chain & Tariff Friction: As a high-volume, low-margin operator (gross margins ~11.0%), global tariff spikes or trade policy shifts could force Costco to choose between absorbing wholesale cost increases or raising prices, potentially risking member price trust.
  • International Store Expansion Friction: Expanding into complex foreign markets (e.g., China, Europe) entails land acquisition challenges, local regulatory hurdles, and supply chain localization risks that could slow the planned cadence of ~26 net new openings per year.
  • SG&A Deleveraging: Wage inflation for warehouse staff and rising logistics costs present ongoing pressure on Costco’s lean operating cost structure (SG&A ratio ~8.96%).

2. Pre-Mortem Falsification Triggers

To guard against confirmation bias, we establish explicit quantitative triggers that would invalidate our thesis and mandate an immediate downgrade or capital reallocation:

Trigger 1: Membership Renewal Erosion Below 90.0%
Costco's membership fees ($1.37B quarterly) generate the vast majority of net operating income. Renewal rates currently stand at 92.2% in the US/Canada and 89.7% globally. A persistent decline in global renewal rates below 90.0% (or US/Canada below 90.5%) would indicate member churn, loss of price leadership, or digital disintermediation, directly breaking the core engine of cash flow growth.
Trigger 2: Sustained Core Gross Margin Compression (>20 bps)
Excluding gasoline volatility, core-on-core gross margin stability is essential. A sequential contraction exceeding 20–30 basis points over two consecutive quarters caused by unpassable supplier price hikes or aggressive price wars would signal erosion in Costco's purchasing leverage.
Trigger 3: SG&A Deleveraging Exceeding 9.20% of Net Sales
Costco's competitive advantage hinges on extreme operating efficiency, maintaining SG&A expenses below ~9.0% of net sales (currently 8.96%). If SG&A deleverages above 9.20% on a sustained basis without offsetting volume gains, warehouse productivity gains are failing to outpace labor cost inflation.
